**Which customer is “tools ACME”?** You can probably spot Acme Tools. Now try Cedar Works: two customer IDs share that exact name. Both earn a perfect similarity score. Which one gets the invoice?

Let's build a matching queue that exposes those decisions. You'll run [RapidFuzz in DuckDB](/products/extensions/rapidfuzz/), rescue a match that an equality join misses, and catch a “perfect” match that needs a person. Try the three cases below, then reproduce the scores yourself.

## Choose a score for the kind of mess you have

The setup defines `name_key`: lowercase, trim, and collapse repeated whitespace. Accents and punctuation stay intact. Removing them or stripping legal suffixes needs a business rule; normalization can erase meaningful distinctions.

Our hand-checked pairs are leads 101 → customer 2, 102 → 1, and 103 → 4. The first query compares their names. Predict which scorer handles “Blue Bird” better, then run it:

<TutorialExample example={demo.examples[0]} />

`rapidfuzz_token_sort_ratio` sorts words before comparing them. That fixes the first pair. It hurts the last pair: splitting “Bluebird” changes the tokens and their sorted order. The [RapidFuzz scorer documentation](https://rapidfuzz.github.io/RapidFuzz/Usage/fuzz.html) explains these different comparison rules.

We will use token sort below to make its limitations visible. In your own workflow, evaluate scorers against labeled matches and nonmatches. A score of 96 is a similarity measure, **not a 96% probability of identity**.

## Reduce the candidate set before scoring

Comparing every incoming record with every customer creates a cross product. Here that is only 63 pairs. Two tables of 100,000 rows would produce ten billion.

A *blocking rule* selects plausible pairs first. Our example requires equal country codes:

<TutorialExample example={demo.examples[1]} />

The result is **26 candidate pairs instead of 63**. This is a count of comparisons in a small fixture, not a runtime benchmark. The setup's `candidates` view applies that country join and scores each surviving pair. Its `ranked` view uses `row_number()` to choose an order and `lead(score)` to retain the runner-up.

Empty or missing normalized names are excluded from scoring; their incoming records remain in the final queue. Two empty strings should never become a confident match.

Blocking depends on reliable country codes. Normalize missing, stale, or inconsistent codes separately: otherwise the correct match can disappear before scoring.

## Keep the runner-up and the unmatched rows

Our demonstration rules require a score of at least 85 and a gap of at least 10 points from the next candidate. **These are teaching thresholds, not production recommendations.**

<TutorialExample example={demo.examples[2]} />

Lead 104 has no candidate, so its customer, score, and gap are all `NULL`. Lead 103 has a score but no runner-up; only its gap is `NULL`. Cedar Works has two perfect candidates, giving a gap of zero.

The `LEFT JOIN` preserves records that have no candidates. Dropping them would make the output look cleaner while hiding failed matches.

Ordering tied scores by ID is reproducible, but does not resolve identity. Cedar Works stays in review. A lone candidate still needs independent corroboration.

## Test the blocking rule against known answers

The `labels` table is our answer key: 101 → 2, 102 → 1, 103 → 4, 104 → 6, 105 → 7, and 107 → 2. Lead 106 has no match. One known pair never reaches scoring:

<TutorialExample example={demo.examples[3]} />

The result is lead 104: its recorded country is MX, but its labeled customer is in CA. Five of six known pairs survive blocking. Changing the score threshold cannot recover the excluded pair.

For a real evaluation, label matches and nonmatches, measure candidate recall and suggestion accuracy, and inspect a held-out set. This tiny fixture demonstrates failure modes; it cannot estimate production accuracy.

## Find possible duplicates within one table

For duplicate review, compare the customer table with itself and keep only `a.id < b.id`. That removes self-comparisons and mirrored pairs:

<TutorialExample example={demo.examples[4]} />

Customer IDs **7 and 9** both say Cedar Works; review their independent identifiers before merging. Similarity is not transitive: A matching B and B matching C does not establish that A and C are the same customer.

Keep original IDs, strings, and scoring rules so matching decisions stay reversible. Continue with the [RapidFuzz reference](/products/extensions/rapidfuzz/) or [Query.Farm consulting](/consulting/) for larger integrations.
